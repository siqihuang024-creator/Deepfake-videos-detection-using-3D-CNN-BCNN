CelebDF++ and DFD Frame Sizing

The input resolution each dataset is mapped to, the transformation applied to reach it, and how that resolution is handled in the two cross-dataset settings.

SummaryOne canvas for all four settings

Specification

Every video in both datasets is resized to a single 768 × 432 canvas: scaled to fit while preserving aspect ratio, then padded on the short side with replicated edge pixels. No face detection, no cropping. The whole frame is kept.

The same transformation is applied in training, validation and testing, and in all four train/test combinations. Only the data source differs between settings.

The rest of this document gives the measurements behind the choice of 768×432, the argument for a single canvas rather than one per dataset, and one confound that the choice does not remove.

2Choosing 768 × 432

The canvas has to leave the face large enough to carry the evidence. Two reference points from earlier runs fix the scale:

  • 149 px — the face size under the face-cropped pipeline used through run v15, which reached 0.7774 test AUROC (0.7226 on the resolution-matched subset).
  • 47 px — the face size when run v12 used the whole frame at 224×224. All eight FaceSwap methods collapsed from 0.742 to 0.547, essentially chance. Blending seams are two to three pixels wide and did not survive.

256×256 is not viable. It puts a CelebDF++ face at 50 px and a DFD face at 27 px, both at or below the size at which v12's face-swap detection collapsed.

768×432 keeps the face at 151 px — the same detail the face-cropped pipeline had — while retaining the whole scene. A 16:9 canvas rather than a square one because every real video and every DFD video is close to 16:9: a square canvas would spend 44% of its pixels on padding for exactly those videos.

3Why one canvas rather than one per dataset

A per-dataset canvas — say 256×256 for CelebDF++ and native 1920×1080 for DFD — is coherent for settings 1 and 2, where each model only ever sees one scale. It breaks in settings 3 and 4 for two reasons.

Convolution kernels have a fixed pixel scale. A 5×5 kernel spans a different physical extent at each resolution. A model trained where faces are 50 px wide and tested where they are 27 px is being asked to read structures at roughly half the size it learned. The resulting number would measure scale mismatch, not generalisation.

Table 5Face size seen in training versus at cross-dataset test, if each dataset kept its own canvas.

† The residual 1.8× under a single canvas is not a preprocessing artefact: DFD frames people at full length (face 10.7% of frame width) while CelebDF++ frames them at half length (19.7%). That is a genuine domain difference between the two corpora, and it is the difference cross-dataset evaluation is supposed to expose. The per-dataset variants add a second, artificial mismatch on top of it.

Training DFD at native resolution is not affordable. The first convolution over a 1920×1080 input produces 43× the activations of a 224×224 input. An epoch would go from roughly 3 minutes to over 2 hours, and a 60-epoch run from 4 hours to more than a week.

5What this does not fix

Confound

In CelebDF++, aspect ratio is correlated with the label: 99.8% of real videos are wide, and 59.6% of fakes are square. Under any fit-and-pad scheme the padded fraction therefore carries class information — 0.2–5.6% for reals against 43.8% for the square fakes.

The same thirteen methods also frame their subject differently: a face fills 58–64% of their frame width against 17–20% for the reals and for the eight FaceSwap methods. Proportional resizing cannot change that ratio, because it multiplies face and frame by the same factor.

No choice of canvas removes either signal. A square canvas pads the wide videos instead of the square ones; an intermediate aspect pads both, on different axes. The confound is a property of the corpus, not of the preprocessing.

Proposed handling

Train on all 22 forgery methods, and report results stratified by whether a method's output matches the reals' framing:

  • Composition-matched subset — the 8 FaceSwap methods plus LivePortrait, all native wide, 996 test videos. This is the headline number.
  • Square subset — the remaining 13 methods, reported separately with the framing difference stated.

Run v15 supports training on everything rather than filtering: restricting training to the 9 matched methods gave 0.7047 on the matched test subset against 0.7226 for training on all 22 — a difference of 0.018, not significant across the 12 test identities. Training with the shortcut available did not damage what the model learned; it only inflated the aggregate number. Stratified reporting is therefore sufficient, and the full dataset can be used.

Question
